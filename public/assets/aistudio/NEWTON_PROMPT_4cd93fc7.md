# Pure-Python 3D-grounded historical cinematic animation pipeline

Use this five-stage workflow to write, render, animate, audit at `1:1` pixel zoom, and refine a `2048 x 1152`, 24 fps, two-minute hand-painted historical film in pure Python about the life of Sir Isaac Newton. Create a visually extraordinary theatrical 2D animated short for the web, telling the story of a man who discovered that the falling of an apple and the motion of the heavens could be understood through the same physical laws. It must look like finished, traditionally drawn animation, not a 3D game cinematic, an animated slideshow, a moving illustration, an educational infographic, or a technology advertisement. Deliver the final result as a single self-contained HTML file with synchronized sound, plus a full-quality 16:9 MP4. Pre-rendering and embedding the film is allowed and encouraged. The page should be a black, letterboxed cinema frame with one quiet play button and nothing else on screen. All artwork and audio must be original, with zero image models, zero video models, and zero external image assets. Historical references may be consulted for accuracy. Use the atmospheric visual language of traditional Japanese theatrical animation and seventeenth-century European painting as references for composition, character design, painted backgrounds, lighting and color, without copying existing artwork or shots. Use 3D for layout and physical simulation where necessary, but make the final result look hand-drawn. There must be absolutely no narration, commentary, dialogue, singing, or explanatory voice-over. Let imagery, movement, visual metaphors, music and restrained environmental sound tell the story.

## THE FILM: “NEWTON — THE HIDDEN ORDER” (120 seconds, 24 fps)

The idea: An apple falls. Light passes through glass. The Moon moves across the sky. These are ordinary events to most people, but to Newton they become clues to the hidden mathematical order of nature. Follow him from an isolated rural childhood to Cambridge, his years of discovery, his scientific publications, and his later life. Begin with the intimate scale of a farmhouse and gradually expand to the scale of the solar system. Balance wonder with solitude, labor, uncertainty and the passage of time. The audience should understand the essential scientific ideas without hearing a single spoken word.

**Shot 1, A World Before Him (0:00–0:09).** Black. Winter wind comes first. Fade up on the snow-covered English countryside at dawn. Bare oaks stand against rolling hills; smoke rises from distant chimneys. Woolsthorpe Manor emerges from pale blue mist, with a single amber window. Move inside to a wooden cradle and a newborn child beside a candle. Introduce a three-note felt-piano motif. Discreet ink lettering establishes Woolsthorpe, England, 1642 (Old Style). Let the warmth of the room contrast with the immense cold landscape.

**Shot 2, The Curious Child (0:09–0:19).** A seasonal match cut reveals the young Isaac. He sits by a farmhouse window constructing a small wooden mechanism, surrounded by handmade models, sketches and measuring instruments. Cut from a miniature windmill turning in his hands to a full-size mill across the countryside. Rain travels down the glass. His eyes follow a droplet as it merges with another. He is quiet, absorbed and intensely observant. Use these scenes as imaginative reconstructions of his childhood interests, not claims of precisely documented incidents.

**Shot 3, Cambridge (0:19–0:30).** Reveal Trinity College in 1661: weathered stone, Gothic windows, wet cobblestones, moving scholars and distant mist. Newton enters carrying books. Cut to a candlelit study with individually painted books, mathematical manuscripts and astronomical diagrams. A montage of ink-stained fingers, carefully drawn geometry, turned pages and melting candles shows the passage of nights. The camera follows a quill stroke into an elegant geometric construction before returning to the room. His posture changes from curiosity to concentration.

**Shot 4, The Silent Return (0:30–0:40).** In 1665, plague disrupts university life. Cambridge becomes quiet: closed doors, empty courtyards, distant bells, abandoned streets. Newton returns to Woolsthorpe. Show him walking across a vast countryside beneath dark clouds, almost swallowed by the landscape. The score contracts to a lone cello and the faint sound of footsteps. Match cut from the college door closing to the farmhouse door opening. Avoid sensational or graphic depictions of disease.

**Shot 5, The Falling Apple (0:40–0:53).** A quiet afternoon beneath an apple tree. Newton studies in the grass. Leaves turn in the sunlight; insects drift through the air. One apple detaches, rotates and falls naturally to the ground. Hold on Newton looking at it, then toward the Moon. Do not make the apple strike his head or depict this as the instant he completed his theory. Use a visual match cut between the apple's curved surface and Earth, then show a falling trajectory gradually becoming a curved orbital path. The same inward acceleration bends both paths. The Moon is continually falling toward Earth while moving sideways. Let the connection emerge slowly, as a question rather than an instant revelation.

**Shot 6, The Mathematics of Change (0:53–1:04).** A candlelit desk. Newton draws a curve on parchment. A point travels along it, and a delicate tangent changes with the point. A sequence of increasingly narrow geometric strips reveals the accumulation of an area. Match these living ink constructions to a flowing stream, a moving pendulum and a turning wheel. Return to Newton crossing out mistakes and beginning again. Convey his development of fluxions and related calculus ideas through movement rather than equations floating over the screen. Do not imply that he was the sole inventor of calculus; a discreet visual reference to the independent work of Leibniz may be included if it improves clarity without breaking the film's pacing.

**Shot 7, The Nature of Light (1:04–1:15).** Almost total darkness. Newton closes a wooden shutter, leaving a narrow aperture. A brilliant ray of sunlight crosses a dusty room and enters a triangular glass prism. The spectrum spreads across the opposite wall in a physically coherent sequence of colors. Show a correctly arranged second optical setup recombining separated colors into white light. His eyes reflect the spectrum. For a moment, the frame opens into a beautiful abstract landscape of painted spectral color, then returns to the quiet room. Harp harmonics and upper strings emerge from the score. Make the optics beautiful because they are geometrically convincing, not because a generic rainbow overlay has been applied.

**Shot 8, Beyond the Earth (1:15–1:29).** Newton constructs and adjusts an early reflecting telescope, circa 1668. Show the curved mirror, brass fittings and tube with plausible period construction. He looks through the instrument. Transition into a vast painted lunar landscape with irregular craters and mountains. Pull back to Earth and the Moon in space. Introduce the cannonball thought experiment as an explicitly symbolic visualization of later gravitational reasoning: successive horizontal launches follow longer curved paths, and at orbital speed a projectile continually falls around Earth. Transition into elliptical planetary paths around the Sun. The camera travels through the silent, ancient cosmos as the musical theme expands. Use coherent trajectories; compress distance and time only for cinematic readability.

**Shot 9, The Principia (1:29–1:42).** Return to the 1680s. Newton is older, surrounded by manuscripts. A brief silent exchange with Edmund Halley introduces the work of bringing his ideas to publication. Show revision, calculation and accumulated pages. Intercut three elegant physical demonstrations: a body continuing its motion in the absence of a net force; equal-mass bodies accelerating differently under different applied forces; and two interacting bodies exerting equal and opposite forces on one another. Keep each demonstration visually legible and physically correct. Transition to a historical printing workshop, inked type, paper impressions and a leather-bound volume. Reveal the actual title: *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, 1687. The musical motif reaches its first major resolution.

**Shot 10, The Passing Years (1:42–1:51).** Move into London through layered streets, carriages, stone buildings, smoke and crowds. Show Newton's later work at the Royal Mint through carefully weighed coins, engraved dies and period machinery. Briefly depict his knighthood in 1705. Use a match cut between the child's hands assembling a mechanism and the older scientist examining a delicate instrument. End with an elderly Newton seated beside a window, afternoon light crossing his face. Do not present his whole life as an uninterrupted series of scientific triumphs; the quiet fatigue of years spent working should be visible.

**Shot 11, The Hidden Order (1:51–2:00).** Dissolve from the elderly Newton to the familiar apple tree under an immense star-filled sky. A falling apple, the arc of a pendulum, the spectrum of light and the orbit of the Moon connect through fine ink-drawn geometries. The camera rises beyond the tree, the clouds and Earth into a wide, awe-inspiring vision of the solar system. The original three-note piano theme returns at orchestral scale. Fade to the textured darkness of a manuscript page. Reveal elegant handwritten lettering: **Sir Isaac Newton — 1642–1727** (birth year in the English Old Style calendar). Beneath it: *In the ordinary, he found the laws of the extraordinary.* This is an original film caption, not a quotation attributed to Newton. Hold long enough to read. One soft piano note. Fade completely to black.

## THEATRICAL ANIMATION QUALITY STANDARD

Every frame must feel deliberately composed, illustrated and directed, with a recognizable visual identity throughout the film.

Character design: Create original, historically informed model sheets for Newton as a child, young adult, mature scientist and elderly man. Preserve the same underlying facial structure, eye placement, nose, brow, posture and characteristic mannerisms across ages and angles. Use restrained expressions, carefully posed hands, period clothing and natural anatomy. Avoid generic cartoon faces, robotic movement, modern clothing and identity drift. Supporting people should be individually designed, not cloned figures.

Line art: Confident, delicate hand-inked contours with variable thickness, subtle pressure variation, intentionally simplified detail and clean silhouettes. Use traditional cel-colored character forms with hard-edged shadow shapes. Reserve the richest pigment texture for backgrounds and props, rather than smearing texture over faces.

Backgrounds: Richly detailed gouache and oil-inspired paintings of English countryside, stone buildings, rain, libraries, wooden furniture, scientific instruments, workshops and the night sky. Give materials individually considered color variation, irregularity, weathering and brush marks. Use layered foreground, midground and background depth, with atmospheric perspective instead of uniformly sharp detail.

Lighting: Progress from winter blue and candle amber to grey plague-era streets, warm garden light, spectral brilliance and deep astronomical indigo. Every shadow, highlight, sunbeam and reflective surface should belong to the same spatially coherent lighting setup.

Animation: Authentic hand-drawn timing, largely on twos and threes, with meaningful holds, strong key poses, anticipation, follow-through, overlapping movement and selective fast animation on ones. Avoid constant camera movement, puppet rotations, floating body parts and generic easing. Let the scientific phenomena move with physically plausible timing while allowing expressive cinematic cuts and time compression.

Direction: Alternate intimate close-ups of observation and intellectual labor with wide atmospheric compositions. Use match cuts, hard cuts, visual rhymes and restrained dissolves. Scientific imagery must belong to the film's painted world rather than appearing as a separate textbook animation. Essential dates and book titles may appear sparingly, but do not use narration, explanatory paragraphs, subtitles or modern interface overlays.

## SOUND

Compose a completely original instrumental score synchronized to the 120-second timeline. Begin with wind and near silence, introduce a three-note felt-piano motif, develop it through low strings during Newton's isolation, add delicate upper strings and harp during the optical experiments, and expand into a full orchestral climax during the astronomical sequences. Return to the intimate piano motif at the end. Use meaningful pauses and controlled dynamics, not a constant wall of music. Original environmental sounds may include wind, rain, footsteps, candle crackle, quill scratching, page turns, wooden mechanisms and the printing press. No narration, commentary, dialogue, vocals, borrowed melodies or external audio samples. Synthesize sound from scratch with numerical DSP and mix it into a stereo master. Do not add scientifically misleading literal sound effects to the vacuum of space; any cosmic sound belongs to the non-diegetic score.

- A modern science explainer: No HUDs, floating labels, neon graphs, stock educational icons or excessive equations.
- Amateur work: No empty backgrounds, copied trees, identical books, stiff hands, muddy lighting, placeholders, broken perspective or unfinished shots.

You are free to change artistic staging and technical implementation if doing so improves the film, but preserve the historical subject, approximate two-minute duration, original music, no spoken commentary, procedural asset constraint and scientific integrity. Before delivery, inspect frames from every shot and watch the encoded result for temporal defects. Do not claim a quality standard has been met unless the rendered evidence supports it.

Required tools: Python 3 (`numpy`, `opencv-python`, `pycairo`, `scipy`, `Pillow`) and FFmpeg for encoding and muxing. Additional non-generative numerical libraries are permitted. Fix random seeds (`np.random.default_rng(seed)`) for deterministic rendering. Target `2048 x 1152`, 24 fps, 2,880 frames, stereo audio at 48 kHz.

## Stage 1 — Metric 3D camera rig, historical geometry and near-plane-safe UV homography

2D code paintings fall apart when perspective lines are guessed by hand in screen pixels. Define all major planes, architecture, historical props, scientific instruments and character root positions in right-handed world meters (`+X` right, `+Y` forward, `+Z` up) through a single reusable pinhole `Camera3D`:

- Place `eye` and `target` off-axis (for example, vanishing point around `55%` to `65%` width rather than dead-center `50%`) so one side carries visual mass while the opposite side opens breathing room. Design each shot around an intentional focal point and silhouette.
- Paint planar architectural surfaces (walls, roofs, desks, paving, timber floors, parchment) in rectangular UV arrays first and convert to premultiplied RGBA. Build each `3 x 3` UV-to-screen homography directly from the 3D plane basis (`K @ [R @ e1, R @ e2, R @ (p0 - eye)] @ H_uv_to_plane` rather than guessing screen corners), mask warped pixels by exact ray-plane depth `z_cam > 0.05`, and blend exponential ray-distance fog (`1 - exp(-max(0, dist - fog_start) * fog_density)`).
- Clip 3D line segments and polygons against `z_cam >= 0.05` before projection so geometry crossing behind the lens never wraps across the frame. Use per-pixel depth where interleaved geometry makes painter's ordering insufficient.
- On historic stone and masonry, replace uniform rectangular grids with jittered seed centers in physical meters, perturb lookup coordinates with smooth bicubic noise, and evaluate second-nearest minus nearest Voronoi distance (`d2 - d1`) over a vectorized `3 x 3` cell neighborhood to carve irregular mortar crevices. Vary stone size, pigmentation, erosion and cracks using stable surface-space seeds.
- Construct period-specific reusable assets for leaded windows, beams, bookcases, printing machinery, brass instruments, prism geometry and Newton's reflecting telescope. Establish character model sheets, facial landmarks, pose libraries and consistent body proportions before building shots. Use 3D reference geometry for anatomy and foreshortening, then draw pose-specific 2D contours rather than exposing a 3D mesh.
- Store camera motion, focus, cuts, animation exposures and object transforms in a deterministic timeline indexed by frame. Use cubic spline camera paths with deliberate acceleration and stopping behavior. Hold the camera still when the shot calls for observation rather than movement.

## Stage 2 — Unified 3D light solver, scientific optics and orbital dynamics

Lock a single normalized 3D light travel vector `SUN_DIR` pointing from the sky toward the floor (`SUN_DIR[2] < 0`), or explicit point-light coordinates, before painting any surface. Use the same light geometry for the backgrounds, character shadow shapes, props, particles and scientific apparatus:

- Trace floor and wall coordinates backward along `-SUN_DIR` onto roofs, windows and shutters (`t = z / -SUN_DIR[2]` for a horizontal reference plane, or solve the general ray-plane intersection), using bounded height refinement for pitched or stepped geometry. Align floor light patches, mullion shadows, volumetric shafts and prop cast shadows.
- For candlelit scenes, model localized warm illumination with bounded distance attenuation, depth-aware occlusion and temporally coherent flame modulation. Use restrained, physically grounded bounce light and dust scattering. Do not randomly flicker entire frames.
- On puddles, wet stone and window glass, combine depth-aware reflected environment samples and plane-mirrored quads (`pts - 2 * dot(pts - p0, n) * n`). Use view-angle Fresnel weighting (`F = F0 + (1 - F0) * (1 - cos_theta)**5`) and world-space ripple distortion; prevent reflections from appearing on occluded or dry surfaces.
- For the prism experiments, trace rays through actual triangular geometry using Snell's law (`n1 * sin(theta1) = n2 * sin(theta2)`) and a plausible wavelength-dependent glass model such as `n(lambda) = A + B/lambda**2`, with consistent units and coefficients. Handle total internal reflection when applicable. Validate that the displayed recombination arrangement genuinely converges the separated rays. Use soft volumetric beam edges, restrained dispersion glow and a physically plausible spectral-to-RGB mapping.
- For the falling apple, use locally appropriate gravitational acceleration and integrate a coherent trajectory. For astronomical scenes, evaluate `a = -G*M*r / norm(r)**3`, where `r` is the displacement from the attracting body, using leapfrog or velocity Verlet integration with a stable timestep. Validate bound orbital behavior, avoid arbitrary circular screen-space rotations, and explicitly distinguish symbolic diagrams from literal scales. Keep the Moon's near-side appearance consistent if depicted over multiple views.
- Validate the three laws of motion using simple numerical models: constant velocity under zero net force, `a = F_net / m` for constant mass, and paired interaction forces with `F_AB = -F_BA`. The third-law forces act on different bodies. Do not illustrate equal force as necessarily equal acceleration for unequal masses. Use mathematically valid tangent and accumulation constructions for the calculus sequence.

## Stage 3 — Depth-interleaved 2x PyCairo splines, isotropic gouache dabs and temporal animation

- Split midground structure and organic forms across at least three depth bands from back to front so architecture, furniture, people and plants overlap naturally in 3D space. Interleave characters with foreground and midground props using depth masks, not a single permanently frontmost character layer.
- Render crisp structural linework, pose-specific character contours and organic silhouettes onto `2x` supersampled `cairo.ImageSurface(FORMAT_ARGB32, w * 2, h * 2)` layers. Stroke Catmull-Rom splines with tapered end weights, subtle hand tremor, and perspective width decay (`clip(1 / (1 + z / (fade_dist * 0.45)), 0.25, 1.0)`). Use correct premultiplied alpha during Lanczos downsampling and unpremultiply only when needed.
- Parameterize natural and architectural details (foliage, branches, book spines, roof tiles, hair strands, clothing seams) via per-instance seeded cubic Bézier curves that vary length, droop, orientation and silhouette nicks rather than stamping translated copies of one polygon.
- Break up flat background fills with multi-octave bicubic-upsampled zero-mean unit-variance Gaussian mottling (`fbm_gouache` at balanced square scales `(72, 68)`, `(26, 29)`, `(10.5, 9.5)`, `(4.5, 4.8)`), 80 to 100 randomly angled soft convex-polygon flat-brush dabs at `1/4` resolution per suitable painted region, fine isotropic cold-press paper tooth, pigment pooling and warm bounce light inside deep shadows (`lum < 0.28`). Never apply high-aspect-ratio row or column noise to ordinary painted fills, which creates synthetic striping at `1:1` zoom.
- Build Newton's age variants from shared landmark and proportion data. Author expressive key poses for looking, writing, walking, manipulating optical instruments, breathing and blinking. Construct pose-dependent face outlines, hands and occlusion masks; use actual alternate drawings for major changes of angle and avoid stretching a single face bitmap. Keep eye movements, blinks, hand contacts and props synchronized. Use exposure sheets with explicit held frames, twos, threes and selective animation on ones.
- Animate secondary elements such as grass, leaves, sleeves, hair, smoke, rain, dust and flames with temporally continuous signals and stable per-object seeds. Anchor surface texture and brush patterns to UV or world coordinates. Do not regenerate independent background or character noise per frame. Apply intentional moving film grain only as a separate controlled finishing pass.
- Cache static paintings, texture atlases, projection matrices, depth masks, lighting components, character drawings and held frames. Use dirty regions and reusable compositing layers for moving elements. Render independent shots in bounded parallel workers, subject to available RAM; avoid retaining thousands of full-resolution float32 frames in memory.

## Stage 4 — Foreground circular disc bokeh, 35mm optics, faint analog patina and original sound synthesis

Frame depth with near-camera silhouette elements where composition benefits from them, convolved in premultiplied RGBA using a normalized circular aperture disc kernel (`make_disc_kernel(radius=7.8)` with a subtle spherical-aberration rim). Scale the blur to the shot's focus geometry rather than forcing the same effect onto every composition.

Finish in float32 RGB with restrained 35mm optics and analog transfer steps:

1. Multi-scale highlight halation (`sigma = 6.5, 24, 72 px`, scaled to output resolution) screen-blended above `lum > 0.72` plus explicit glints, using scene-matched warm or cool tints. Preserve the crispness of character linework and optical diagrams.
2. Radial corner chromatic aberration and gentle optical vignette. Keep displacement subpixel to roughly one pixel and avoid visible color separation around text and scientific diagrams.
3. Linear shadow black lift paired with a `C1`-continuous `tanh` highlight shoulder above `knee = 0.80` (`ceiling = 0.965`). Work in a declared color space, perform physical light compositing in linear light, and convert to the intended display transfer function for output. Prevent double gamma correction.
4. Luminance-weighted silver-halide grain (`env = clip(1.12 - 0.95 * lum ** 1.55, 0.12, 1.12)`) so shadows hold organic texture while bright skies stay clean. Use deterministic but temporally controlled grain to avoid aggressive flickering or encoding artifacts.
5. Optional faint analog transfer patina: horizontal YIQ chroma softening (`0.8` to `1.2 px`) with slight luma ring and `cv2.BORDER_REFLECT_101` padding, very restrained subpixel tape jitter, and `<= 0.8%` scanline modulation. Disable any effect that makes the work look like degraded video instead of a beautiful theatrical film.

For original sound, synthesize a stereo `float32` mix at `48,000 Hz` using a shot-synchronized sample timeline. Build a reusable original three-note leitmotif and develop it through changing harmony, voicing, tempo and orchestration. Use additive or modal synthesis for felt piano and strings, shaped noise and resonant filters for wind and paper, filtered transients for quill strokes and mechanisms, and carefully controlled attack/decay envelopes for all sounds. Introduce stereo positioning, room-specific convolution or algorithmic reverberation, and phrase-level dynamic automation. Render instruments and sound effects as separate stems before mixing. Avoid conspicuously synthetic looping, clipping, abrupt audio discontinuities and borrowed melodies. Target approximately `-16 LUFS` integrated as an adjustable web playback reference and `-1 dBTP` maximum true peak, preserving cinematic dynamic contrast. End the final audio sample at the exact film duration; no speech or vocals.
